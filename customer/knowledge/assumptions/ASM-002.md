title: What the fifty-address response cap assumes, none of it measured

> **Draft, reconciled from a hotfix.** Recorded so the beliefs behind
> [BR-012](../rules/BR-012.md) are visible and can be argued with, not because
> any of them are known to be true.

Commit `30d52e6` capped the address list at fifty during an incident. The commit
message gives one sentence of cause and the diff gives none. Four things were
assumed rather than established, and each of them is separately checkable by
someone who is not at a keyboard at 2am.

1. **That the number of addresses caused the timeout.** The commit asserts a
   customer with thousands of addresses was timing out the page. No timing, no
   trace and no address count is recorded anywhere in the change. Serialising a
   few thousand small objects is not obviously slow, so the cause may equally
   have been a query, an N+1 in the page that consumes this list, or something
   unrelated that happened at the same time. If the cause was elsewhere, this
   cap changed the contract for nothing.

2. **That fifty is enough for every caller.** Nothing identifies who calls this
   endpoint or what they do with the result. A caller that renders a picker
   tolerates truncation; a caller that reconciles, exports, counts, or looks an
   address up by line silently gets a wrong answer, and gets it with a 200.

3. **That silent truncation is safer than failing.** The change chose to return
   a wrong-but-fast answer over a slow-but-complete one, and to say nothing
   about the difference. Pagination, a total count, a 413, or an explicit
   `truncated` flag would each have preserved the caller's ability to tell.
   None of these appear in the diff, and nothing suggests they were weighed, so
   this is recorded here as an assumption rather than in a decision record. A
   decision record listing rejected alternatives would be a fiction: the diff
   contains no evidence that any alternative was considered.

4. **That customers holding more than fifty addresses exist at all.**
   [BR-011](../rules/BR-011.md) caps the address book at twenty, and
   [ASM-001](ASM-001.md) records that the limit was assumed non-retroactive
   because "nothing in this system can currently produce a customer holding more
   than twenty". Either that assumption is wrong and there is an unrecorded path
   that creates addresses without checking the limit, or the incident this
   hotfix responded to cannot have been what the commit message says it was.
   Both possibilities are serious and neither is resolved. This is the single
   most important thing to establish before deciding anything about
   [BR-012](../rules/BR-012.md).

If (4) resolves as "no such customer exists", then BR-012 describes code that
can never execute, and the correct outcome of this reconciliation is a revert
rather than a rule.
